A difficult experience can be over and still feel as though it is happening now.

You may know that you are safe, yet find yourself reacting to reminders as if the danger is still present. Perhaps certain sounds, places or situations bring everything back. You might experience nightmares, unwanted images, a sense of being constantly on guard, or a strong urge to avoid anything connected to what happened.

At other times, the effect is less obvious. You may feel numb or disconnected, find it difficult to trust people, become overwhelmed more easily than you used to, or notice that your body reacts before you have had time to think.

EMDR is a structured therapy that can help people process traumatic experiences so that the memories feel more firmly rooted in the past.

## When the mind's alarm system stays switched on

After a frightening or overwhelming event, it is natural for the mind and body to respond strongly. In the days and weeks afterwards, many people find that these reactions gradually settle.

Sometimes they do not.

A memory may continue to carry the same fear, helplessness, shame or horror that was present at the time. Something in everyday life can then activate the brain's alarm system, even when the current situation is safe.

This can be confusing. One part of you knows the event is over, while another part seems not to have received that message.

Post-traumatic stress disorder, or PTSD, is one way these difficulties may be understood. You do not need to arrive at therapy with a diagnosis, however. We can begin with what you are experiencing and how it is affecting your life now.

## Trauma is not always one dramatic event

Some people seek EMDR after a single experience such as an accident, assault, medical emergency or sudden loss.

For others, the impact has developed through experiences that happened repeatedly over time. This might include bullying, abuse, neglect, an unpredictable home environment, unsafe relationships or repeatedly being made to feel powerless.

People sometimes minimise these experiences because somebody else had it worse, or because there is no single event that explains everything.

But the effect of an experience is not measured by how dramatic it sounds from the outside. What matters is what happened to you, how you experienced it, and whether it is still influencing your thoughts, feelings, relationships or sense of safety.

## How can EMDR help?

EMDR does not erase a memory or ask you to pretend that what happened was acceptable.

The aim is to help the brain process an experience that still feels stuck. During the processing stages of EMDR, you bring part of a memory to mind while engaging in bilateral stimulation. This may involve following hand movements with your eyes, tapping, or listening to sounds that alternate from side to side.

We may work with the image that feels most difficult, the emotions and physical sensations connected to it, and beliefs such as *“I am not safe,” “It was my fault,”* or *“I am powerless.”*

As processing takes place, people often find that the memory begins to feel less immediate and less emotionally charged. It remains something that happened, but it may no longer take over in the same way.

## Do I have to describe everything in detail?

Not necessarily.

I need enough understanding to work with you safely and appropriately, but EMDR does not depend on giving a detailed, blow-by-blow account of everything that happened.

Nor would I expect you to begin by going straight into the most difficult experience. We spend time understanding what is happening for you, identifying the memories or situations that may need attention, and preparing for the work.

You remain in control throughout. We agree how you can pause, slow down or stop, and we regularly check what you are noticing. The purpose is not to overwhelm you. It is to help you approach difficult material in a way that feels manageable.

## Looking beyond the original experience

Trauma can affect more than the memory itself. It may change how you respond to present-day situations and what you expect will happen in the future.

EMDR can therefore look at all three:

- experiences from the past that may be driving the difficulty
- triggers and patterns that affect you now
- future situations in which you would like to feel safer or respond differently

The work is adapted to the individual. A single event and a long history of repeated experiences are unlikely to require exactly the same approach or amount of time.

## What does the research say?

EMDR has its strongest evidence base as a treatment for PTSD. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends EMDR for many adults with PTSD or clinically important symptoms of PTSD. The World Health Organization also includes EMDR among its recommended psychological treatments for PTSD.

Further reading:

- [NICE: Post-traumatic stress disorder—recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng116/chapter/recommendations)
- [World Health Organization: Guidelines on conditions specifically related to stress](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241505406)

## Is EMDR right for me?

EMDR is a well-established treatment for PTSD, but that does not mean it is automatically the right starting point for everyone who has experienced trauma.

Your current circumstances, safety, support, health and hopes for therapy all matter. Sometimes preparation and stabilisation need to come first. Sometimes another form of therapy, or a combination of approaches, may be more suitable.

You do not need to work that out by yourself.
