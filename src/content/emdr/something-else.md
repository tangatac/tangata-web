People do not always arrive at therapy with a clear label for what is wrong.

You may know that certain situations affect you more strongly than you would like. Perhaps the same pattern keeps appearing in relationships, a difficult memory returns unexpectedly, or you feel held back by shame, guilt, anger or an urge that is hard to understand.

You might also recognise parts of several different descriptions without feeling that any one of them quite fits.

That is okay. Therapy can begin with your experience rather than a diagnosis.

## EMDR is not only about labels

EMDR is best known for its use with trauma and PTSD. It can also be considered when past experiences appear to be contributing to a present-day difficulty.

Depending on the person, this might involve:

- distressing or intrusive memories
- strong reactions to particular triggers
- shame or guilt connected to something that happened
- relationship patterns shaped by earlier experiences
- fears about a future situation
- certain unwanted urges or compulsive behaviours
- a persistent negative belief about yourself

This does not mean that every difficulty is caused by trauma, or that EMDR is the answer to everything on the list.

The same outward problem can have very different roots. Two people may both struggle with anger, for example, while one is reacting to a recent situation and the other is being pulled into an older sense of threat. Understanding that difference helps us decide what kind of therapy may be useful.

## Starting with what happens now

You do not need to uncover a forgotten memory or arrive with a complete explanation.

We can begin with what you notice in the present:

- When does the difficulty tend to happen?
- What thoughts, feelings or physical sensations come with it?
- What do you find yourself doing to cope?
- How is it affecting your life or relationships?
- What would you like to be different?

From there, we can explore whether earlier experiences seem relevant. Sometimes the connection is clear. Sometimes it develops gradually through the work. Sometimes the most helpful focus remains firmly in the present.

## EMDR, counselling or both?

My work is integrative, which means I do not need to make every difficulty fit one method.

EMDR may be useful when there is a specific memory, trigger, belief or feared situation to work with. Counselling may offer more space to understand yourself, explore a current problem or experience a different kind of relationship. Often, elements of both can sit alongside each other.

We may also decide that another service or specialist would be better placed to help. If that is the case, I will be open with you about it.

The aim of an initial conversation is not to persuade you into EMDR. It is to begin making sense of what you need.

## What if I cannot explain it properly?

You do not have to.

Many people begin therapy by saying, *“I don't really know where to start,”* or *“It doesn't sound like a big thing, but it is affecting me.”*

There is no perfect way to tell your story. We can take time, ask questions and notice together what seems important. You will not be expected to share every detail in the first session, and you remain in control of the pace.

If EMDR becomes part of the work, preparation comes before processing. We agree ways to pause and stay grounded, and we only move towards difficult material when there is a shared understanding of why we are doing so.

## What does the research say?

EMDR has a substantial evidence base and is recommended in clinical guidelines for PTSD. It has also been studied with a wider range of difficulties, but the amount and quality of evidence vary considerably. That is why I would not assume that EMDR is the right treatment simply because a difficulty appears on a list.

Further reading:

- [NICE: Post-traumatic stress disorder—recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng116/chapter/recommendations)
- [Cuijpers and colleagues (2020): Systematic review of EMDR for mental health problems](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32043428/)

## The first step is a conversation

It can be difficult to decide which kind of therapy you need from a list on a website.

You do not need to diagnose yourself or be certain that your problem is serious enough. If something is getting in the way of the life, relationships or sense of ease you would like to have, we can talk about it.

Together, we can consider whether EMDR, integrative counselling or another form of support might be the most appropriate next step.
