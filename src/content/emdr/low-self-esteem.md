Low self-esteem can make it difficult to see yourself fairly.

You may focus on mistakes while dismissing the things you do well. Praise might feel uncomfortable or impossible to believe. Perhaps you compare yourself constantly, expect criticism, find it hard to set boundaries, or feel that you need to earn your place in relationships.

On one level, you may know that you are capable or worthy of care. Yet another part of you continues to say, *“I am not good enough,” “There is something wrong with me,”* or *“Other people matter more than I do.”*

EMDR can sometimes help when beliefs like these are connected to earlier experiences that still shape how you see yourself now.

## We are not born with an inner critic

The beliefs we hold about ourselves develop over time.

A single humiliating or frightening event can have an impact. More often, low self-esteem grows through repeated experiences: criticism, bullying, rejection, neglect, discrimination, feeling different, being compared with other people, or learning that approval depended on getting everything right.

These experiences do not have to look dramatic from the outside. A message received quietly and repeatedly can become deeply familiar.

You may have adapted by becoming highly self-reliant, avoiding attention, pleasing other people or working relentlessly to prevent criticism. Those strategies may once have helped you feel safer or more accepted. Later, they can become exhausting and restrictive.

## Knowing something is not always the same as feeling it

People with low self-esteem are often very aware that their self-criticism is harsher than it needs to be.

Friends may reassure you. You may be able to list evidence that you are competent, kind or valued. But when something goes wrong, the old belief returns with surprising force.

That gap between logical knowledge and emotional response is important.

If an earlier experience has not been fully processed, it can continue to carry the feelings and conclusions that were present at the time. A small mistake now may activate an old sense of shame. A disagreement may feel like proof that you will be rejected. A new opportunity may bring up the expectation that you will be exposed as not good enough.

## How might EMDR help?

EMDR can be used to work with memories that contributed to a negative belief about yourself.

We might begin with a particular event, such as being ridiculed at school, criticised by a parent, excluded by a group or treated badly in a relationship. We pay attention not only to what happened, but also to the meaning you took from it and the way it is felt in the body.

During processing, bilateral stimulation is used while you notice aspects of the experience. As the memory becomes less emotionally charged, the old conclusion may also begin to loosen.

This is not about replacing one rigid statement with forced positive thinking. Therapy cannot simply instruct you to believe that you are good enough.

The aim is to help more balanced beliefs feel emotionally possible: *“I did not deserve that,” “I can make mistakes and still be accepted,”* or *“I have choices now.”*

## Working with present patterns and future situations

Low self-esteem often shows up in everyday patterns as well as memories.

You may apologise when you have done nothing wrong, struggle to say no, remain in situations that do not meet your needs, or hold back from things you would like to do. We can explore what triggers those responses and what you fear might happen if you acted differently.

EMDR can also look towards future situations. You might imagine expressing a need, receiving feedback, entering a social setting or taking on a challenge while practising a different way of responding.

Change is not about becoming confident all the time. It may begin with treating yourself more fairly, recovering more quickly from setbacks or having a little more freedom to choose.

## The relationship matters too

Low self-esteem usually developed in relation to other people, and the therapeutic relationship can be an important part of healing.

You will not be expected to perform, say the right thing or make progress according to somebody else's timetable. We begin by understanding your experiences and what helps you feel safe enough to be honest.

Depending on your needs, our work may combine EMDR with integrative counselling. Sometimes it is helpful to process particular memories. At other times, talking, reflecting and experiencing a consistent relationship are just as important.

## What does the research say?

EMDR is an evidence-based treatment for PTSD, but there is not an equivalent body of research supporting it for low self-esteem as a standalone difficulty. Its use here is more individual: working with particular distressing experiences and negative beliefs when these appear to be shaping how somebody sees themselves now.

## Is EMDR right for me?

Not every struggle with confidence comes from a traumatic memory, and EMDR is not automatically the right approach for low self-esteem.

It may be helpful where past experiences continue to carry strong shame, fear or negative beliefs about who you are. We would consider that together rather than assuming the answer in advance.

You do not need to know exactly where your self-doubt began. We can start with how it affects you now and what you would like to be different.
