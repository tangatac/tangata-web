A phobia is more than simply disliking something.

It can create a strong, immediate fear response that feels difficult to control, even when you know the actual risk is small. You may avoid certain places, objects, animals, journeys, medical procedures or situations. Sometimes the effort involved in avoiding the fear begins to shape everyday life.

People often feel embarrassed by this. They may think, *“I know it doesn't make sense, so why can't I just get over it?”*

But fear does not operate through logic alone. EMDR can sometimes help by working with the experiences and associations that keep the mind's alarm response in place.

## When fear takes over

A phobic response can happen very quickly.

You might notice a racing heart, dizziness, nausea, trembling, an urge to escape, or a feeling of being frozen. The body reacts first, while the calmer, more logical part of the mind struggles to catch up.

Avoidance can bring understandable short-term relief. If you do not get on the plane, enter the lift or make the appointment, the immediate fear reduces.

The difficulty is that avoidance can also confirm the message that the situation was too dangerous to face. Over time, the fear may stay the same or begin to spread into other parts of life.

## Do I need to know where the phobia began?

Not always.

Some people can trace a phobia to a particular event: being bitten by a dog, becoming trapped, experiencing severe turbulence, having a painful procedure or witnessing somebody else in distress.

For others, there is no single clear starting point. The fear may have developed gradually, been learnt early in life, or become attached to a physical sensation such as breathlessness or nausea.

We can explore what happens for you now without assuming that there must be one hidden cause. If relevant memories do emerge, they can become part of the work.

## How might EMDR help with a phobia?

EMDR may be used to process an experience that contributed to the fear, as well as the present-day triggers that continue to activate it.

For example, if a frightening flight sits behind a fear of flying, we might work with the most distressing part of that memory, the sensations it brings up and beliefs such as *“I am trapped”* or *“I cannot cope.”*

If there is no obvious event, we may begin with the way the fear shows up now: an image, anticipated catastrophe, bodily sensation or moment when the anxiety is at its strongest.

Processing uses bilateral stimulation within a wider, structured therapy. The aim is to reduce the emotional charge attached to the memory or trigger, so that the present situation can be experienced more as it is now rather than through the full force of earlier fear.

## Preparing for future situations

Phobias are not only about what has happened before. They are also shaped by what you imagine will happen next.

Therapy may therefore include thinking about a future situation that you would like to manage differently. This could be attending an appointment, travelling, encountering an animal or remaining in a place that previously felt impossible.

We can explore what you fear might happen, what you would like to believe about your ability to cope, and what a manageable next step might look like.

Depending on the phobia and your needs, gradual, agreed practice in real-life situations may also form part of the wider work. This would be planned collaboratively, not imposed on you or used to catch you out.

## Will I be made to face my fear straight away?

No.

We begin by understanding the fear, its impact and what helps you feel steady. Preparation is an important part of EMDR, and you remain in control of the pace throughout.

The purpose of therapy is not to prove that you should be able to tolerate being overwhelmed. It is to help your mind and body respond differently, with enough support and preparation for the work to feel manageable.

## What does the research say?

EMDR is firmly established for PTSD, while research into specific phobias is more limited. A 2020 meta-analysis of randomised trials found a reduction in phobia symptoms, but a separate broad review noted that the number and quality of studies outside PTSD were still limited. I therefore treat the research as promising rather than conclusive.

Further reading:

- [Yunitri and colleagues (2020): Meta-analysis of EMDR for anxiety disorders](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32058073/)
- [Cuijpers and colleagues (2020): Systematic review of EMDR for mental health problems](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32043428/)

## Is EMDR right for my phobia?

EMDR can be helpful for some people with specific fears, particularly where a distressing experience or vivid feared outcome appears to be maintaining the response. It is not the only effective way to work with phobias, and the best approach will depend on you, the nature of the fear and how it affects your life.

We can talk about what you avoid, what happens when the fear is triggered, and what you would like to be able to do. From there, we can consider whether EMDR, counselling or another approach would be most appropriate.
