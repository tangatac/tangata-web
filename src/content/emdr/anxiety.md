Anxiety is part of being human. It is the mind and body's way of noticing possible danger and preparing us to respond.

The difficulty comes when that alarm system is activated too often, too strongly, or in situations where there is no immediate threat.

You may find yourself going over conversations, imagining everything that could go wrong, avoiding particular situations, or looking for reassurance that only helps briefly. Anxiety can also be very physical: a tight chest, racing heart, unsettled stomach, tense muscles or a sense that you cannot properly switch off.

Sometimes there is an obvious reason for it. Sometimes there is not.

EMDR can be helpful when present-day anxiety appears to be connected to earlier experiences, distressing memories or particular triggers.

## When your reaction feels bigger than the situation

One of the frustrating things about anxiety is the gap between what you know and what you feel.

You might know that making a small mistake is unlikely to be a disaster, yet feel intense dread before sending an email. You may understand that a partner is not the person who hurt you in the past, but still expect rejection. Perhaps you know that a meeting, journey or social event is manageable, while your body responds as though you are entering danger.

This does not mean you are being irrational or deliberately making things difficult for yourself.

Our reactions are shaped by experience. A panic attack, public humiliation, frightening event, unpredictable relationship or years of criticism can all influence what the mind learns to treat as threatening.

## Does anxiety always come from the past?

No.

Anxiety can be affected by many things, including current stress, uncertainty, health, relationships and the demands being placed on you. Not every anxious feeling needs to be traced back to childhood or treated as trauma.

Part of therapy is working out what is keeping the anxiety going for you.

For some people, the most helpful focus may be what is happening in life now. For others, there are particular experiences that still carry fear, shame or helplessness. Often it is a mixture of both.

This is why therapy is not simply a matter of matching the word *anxiety* to one particular technique.

## How might EMDR help with anxiety?

EMDR can be used to work with memories and situations that seem to sit underneath an anxious response.

For example, somebody who becomes extremely anxious about speaking in meetings may connect that feeling to an earlier experience of being mocked in front of other people. Someone who fears losing control may remember their first panic attack as if it were evidence that another one is always about to happen.

During EMDR, we may identify the relevant memory, the belief attached to it and the physical response it brings up. Bilateral stimulation is then used as part of a structured process to help the experience become less emotionally charged.

The aim is not to remove every trace of anxiety. Some anxiety is useful. The aim is for your response to become more proportionate, and for old experiences to have less influence over what feels possible now.

## Working with present-day triggers and future situations

EMDR does not only look backwards.

We may also explore what currently activates the anxiety. That could be conflict, uncertainty, being evaluated, travelling, crowded places, physical sensations or something else entirely.

Therapy can then look towards future situations. You might imagine approaching something that previously felt difficult while noticing what you would like to think, feel and do differently.

This does not guarantee that you will never feel anxious again. It can, however, help create a greater sense of choice, so that anxiety is not making every decision for you.

## What happens before processing begins?

We begin by getting to know you and understanding the anxiety in context.

We will consider when it happens, what seems to trigger it, how you currently manage it and what you would like to change. If EMDR seems appropriate, we also spend time on preparation and ways of staying grounded before working with more difficult material.

You remain in control of the pace. You can pause at any point, and you will not be expected to disclose every detail of an experience before you are ready.

## What does the research say?

The most established evidence for EMDR is in the treatment of PTSD. Research into anxiety is smaller but developing. A 2020 meta-analysis of 17 randomised controlled trials reported reductions in anxiety, panic and phobia symptoms, while also noting that more research was needed into longer-term outcomes.

Further reading:

- [Yunitri and colleagues (2020): Meta-analysis of EMDR for anxiety disorders](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32058073/)
- [NICE: Post-traumatic stress disorder—recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng116/chapter/recommendations)

## Is EMDR the right approach for my anxiety?

That depends on the individual and what appears to be driving the difficulty.

EMDR may be useful when anxiety is linked to distressing memories, learnt fears or experiences that still feel unresolved. At other times, integrative counselling or a different therapeutic approach may make more sense. Different approaches can also be used together.
