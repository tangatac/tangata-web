Grief is not an illness to be cured. It is a natural response to losing somebody or something that mattered.

There is no correct timetable and no single way that grief should look. It may involve sadness, anger, guilt, relief, numbness, longing or a mixture that changes from one day to the next.

Most people do not need therapy simply because they are grieving. Sometimes, however, a particular part of what happened can feel so distressing that it leaves little room for anything else.

EMDR may help when painful memories, images or circumstances surrounding a loss continue to feel immediate and overwhelming.

## Loss can take many forms

Bereavement is perhaps what we most often mean when we talk about grief, but loss can also follow the end of a relationship, a change in health, redundancy, infertility, estrangement, migration or the loss of a role, future or way of life.

Some losses are recognised and supported by other people. Others are private, complicated or difficult to explain.

You may feel that you should be coping by now, or that you do not have the right to grieve as deeply as you do. Those judgements can add another layer of pain to an already difficult experience.

Therapy offers a place where the loss does not need to be compared, justified or hurried.

## When grief and trauma become tangled together

Grief is painful, but pain alone does not mean that something has gone wrong.

At times, though, the way a loss happened can be traumatic. You may be troubled by the moment you received the news, an image of somebody suffering, a medical emergency, a sudden death, or a decision you had to make.

The mind may repeatedly return to questions such as *“What if I had done something differently?”* or *“Why didn't I see it coming?”* You might avoid reminders, feel unable to speak about what happened, or experience the memory as though you are back in that moment.

When this happens, the most distressing aspects of the loss can dominate the wider relationship and everything else you remember.

## How might EMDR help?

EMDR does not aim to remove grief, erase memories or weaken the bond you had with the person who died.

It may help process the experiences that feel stuck around the loss. We might work with a distressing image, a moment of helplessness, or beliefs such as *“It was my fault,” “I should have prevented it,”* or *“I cannot cope without them.”*

During processing, bilateral stimulation is used while you notice parts of the memory, the emotions and any physical sensations that arise. Over time, the memory may begin to feel less immediate and less overwhelming.

This can allow other memories, feelings and meanings to sit alongside the most painful part of what happened. The loss still matters. The aim is for you to be able to remember without always being pulled back into the worst moment.

## What if I am worried about letting go?

People sometimes worry that feeling less distressed means forgetting, moving on too quickly or leaving somebody behind.

That is understandable.

Processing a traumatic memory is not the same as letting go of a person or pretending the loss no longer matters. EMDR works with the distress surrounding an experience; it does not decide what the relationship should mean to you.

There is room in therapy for continuing bonds, mixed feelings and the ways a loss becomes part of your life story. The work is not about reaching a point where you never feel sad. It is about helping grief become something you can carry, rather than something that repeatedly overwhelms you.

## Grief needs its own pace

There would be no expectation that you begin processing the most difficult memory straight away.

We first spend time understanding the loss, your current circumstances, the support around you and what you hope therapy might offer. Preparation and grounding are important, particularly when emotions feel very close to the surface.

For some people, EMDR becomes part of the work. For others, the most helpful thing is a counselling relationship in which there is time to talk, remember and make sense of what has changed. The two approaches can also be combined.

You remain in control of what we focus on and how quickly we proceed.

## What does the research say?

Research into EMDR for grief is much smaller than the evidence base for PTSD. Early studies suggest it may be helpful for distressing or traumatic aspects of bereavement, but the evidence is still developing. A 2024 peer-reviewed review brings together the current research and also highlights the need for larger studies and longer follow-up.

Further reading:

- [Spicer (2024): EMDR therapy for prolonged grief—theory, research and practice](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2024.1357390)
- [NICE: Post-traumatic stress disorder—recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng116/chapter/recommendations)

## Could therapy help me?

There is no test you need to pass and no point at which grief becomes worthy of support.

You may be struggling with a recent loss, or something that happened a long time ago but still feels unfinished. You may know exactly what you need help with, or only know that things have become difficult to carry alone.

We can begin by talking about what has happened and what life is like for you now. Together, we can consider whether EMDR, counselling or a combination of the two would be most helpful.
