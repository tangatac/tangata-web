When people hear about EMDR, one of the first questions they often ask me is: **“Is it safe?”**

It’s a very reasonable question.

After all, EMDR involves working with experiences that may have been frightening, overwhelming or deeply upsetting. You might be wondering why you would deliberately go back to something that you have spent years trying not to think about.

The answer is that **safety comes first in EMDR**. Before we start processing difficult memories, a significant part of the work is making sure that you have enough stability, resources and support to do that safely.

It isn't about throwing you into the deep end.

## Making sure you are ready

One of the first things we need to think about is how well resourced you are at the moment.

That doesn't mean that you have to feel completely fine before starting EMDR. If you did, you probably wouldn't be looking for therapy in the first place!

It means looking at what's happening in your life now and asking questions such as:

- How are you managing day to day?

- Do you have people or things that help you feel supported?

- Can you recognise when you're becoming overwhelmed?

- What helps you to calm yourself down?

- How well are you sleeping and functioning?

- Do you have enough stability in your life to begin working with difficult material?

Sometimes the answer will be, **“Yes, I'm ready to work on this.”**

Sometimes it will be, **“We need to spend some more time building up your resources first.”**

And sometimes the most appropriate thing is to focus on what's happening in your life now rather than processing the past.

There isn't a race.

## Keeping one foot in the present

A useful way of thinking about EMDR is that, while we may be looking at something that happened in the past, **you are still here, in the room, in the present.**

I sometimes describe this as having **one foot in the past and one foot in the present**.

We want enough connection with the memory for your brain to be able to process it, but we don't want you to become completely lost in the experience as though it were happening again.

There is an important difference between **remembering something frightening** and **feeling as though you are back there experiencing it now**.

Part of my job is helping you stay connected to the present while we work.

That might mean noticing the chair underneath you, your feet on the floor, the room around you, my presence, or your breathing. It might involve taking a break, changing what we're doing or coming back to something that helps you feel more settled.

And you remain in control.

You can slow things down. You can ask to stop. You can tell me that something feels too much.

## Building resources before processing

Another important part of EMDR is developing what are often called **resources**.

These are things that help you feel calmer, safer, stronger or more in control.

For some people, that might be remembering a person with whom they feel safe. For someone else it could be a particular place, a physical sensation, an activity, music, movement or simply knowing how to bring their attention back into the present.

We practise these things rather than simply hoping you'll be able to use them when things get difficult.

This is particularly important because everyone's capacity to deal with difficult emotions is different. What one person can comfortably tolerate might be far too much for someone else.

So EMDR isn't a standard process where I decide, *“Today we're going to deal with your worst memory.”*

We work collaboratively and pay attention to what your nervous system is telling us.

## What if you become overwhelmed?

An important part of safe EMDR is knowing what to do if things become too intense.

Sometimes people worry that once they start talking about a traumatic experience, they won't be able to stop.

That isn't how I want therapy to work.

If you become overwhelmed, we can pause and reconnect with the present. We can use the resources you've developed. We can change direction. And sometimes we might decide that we're not going to process that particular memory yet.

There may also be times when something unexpected comes up between sessions. That's why the work isn't just about what happens during the EMDR session itself. We also think about how you are likely to manage afterwards and what support you have available.

**The aim isn't to make you suffer through the memory. The aim is to help your brain process something that is still causing you problems now.**

## Why the therapeutic relationship matters

Safety isn't just about techniques.

It also comes from the relationship between therapist and client.

You need to feel that you can say, *“I don't want to do that,”* or *“That's too much,”* without feeling that you're letting your therapist down.

My role isn't to push you further than you want to go. It's to help you work out what is manageable and useful, and to make adjustments when necessary.

EMDR is a structured therapy, but that doesn't mean every person experiences it in exactly the same way.

Your history, your circumstances and your ability to cope in the present all matter.

## You don't have to be completely “fixed” before you start

There's a balance here.

If we waited until everyone was perfectly calm, completely confident and had no difficulties coping before beginning trauma processing, many people would never get started.

The question is not **“Am I completely okay?”**

It's more:

**“Do I have enough stability and resources to do this safely, and can we work together to keep me connected to the present while we do it?”**

Sometimes the answer is yes.

Sometimes we need to spend longer establishing that foundation first.

Either way, that's part of EMDR.

So if you're considering EMDR but are worried about what it might involve, you don't have to arrive knowing that you're ready to process everything.

The first step is simply talking about what's going on and working out together what might be helpful.

**Safety comes before processing.**
