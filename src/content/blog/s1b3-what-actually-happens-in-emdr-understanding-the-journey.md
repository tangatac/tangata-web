If you’ve heard of EMDR, you might have an image in your head of someone sitting in a therapy room, moving their eyes from side to side while thinking about something traumatic.

That is certainly part of EMDR, but it’s not how you start.

In fact, there’s quite a journey before we get to the processing itself. And that journey matters, because EMDR isn’t simply about finding something difficult in your past and making yourself think about it. The aim is to help you process experiences that are still affecting you now, while making sure you have the resources and stability to do that safely.

So, what actually happens?

## The first session: getting to know each other

The first session is an opportunity to talk about what has brought you to counselling and what you would like help with.

We’ll talk about what is happening for you now – perhaps anxiety, panic, low self-esteem, intrusive memories, nightmares, avoidance, relationship difficulties or a particular event that you’re struggling with.

It’s also a chance for you to find out whether EMDR feels like the right approach for you and whether you feel comfortable working with me.

I’ll ask questions about your current circumstances and your history, but you don’t have to arrive with your whole life story neatly organised. We can work it out together.

An important part of these early conversations is understanding not just **what happened**, but **how it is affecting you now**.

That distinction is really important.

## Before processing: safety and stabilisation

If EMDR is appropriate, we don't necessarily move straight into processing memories.

First, we need to make sure you have enough stability and resources to manage the work. This might include learning ways of grounding yourself, managing difficult emotions and coming back into the present when things feel overwhelming.

We might also explore what helps you feel safe, calm and in control.

Think of it a little like preparing for a journey. Before setting off somewhere potentially challenging, you want to know where you're going, what you're likely to encounter and what resources you've got with you.

There's no prize for getting to the difficult memories as quickly as possible.

For some people, this preparation is relatively brief. For others, particularly when there has been a lot going on in their lives or experiences have been complex, we may spend longer building those foundations.

## When regular sessions begin

Once we've established that EMDR is appropriate and you've got some useful resources in place, we'll develop a treatment plan.

This involves looking at the things you're struggling with now and beginning to understand where they might have come from.

This is where EMDR can sometimes be different from what people expect from therapy.

Rather than simply asking, *“What terrible thing happened to you?”*, we can start with what is happening **today**.

For example, perhaps you feel panicky when someone raises their voice. Perhaps you find it difficult to trust people. Maybe you constantly feel that you're not good enough, or you avoid certain situations because they make you anxious.

We'll look at those present-day problems and then begin to explore the experiences connected to them.

## Finding the memories underneath the problem

Once we understand a current problem, we can work backwards.

Sometimes there is an obvious event that started it. If you're frightened after being involved in a car accident, for example, the connection may be fairly straightforward.

But sometimes it's much less obvious.

You might know that you react very strongly to criticism, for instance, without immediately knowing why.

We can gently explore when you first remember feeling that way, or when a particular belief about yourself first appeared.

In EMDR, we sometimes talk about identifying a **“touchstone”** memory – an earlier experience that seems connected to the way you feel or behave now.

It doesn't necessarily have to be the worst thing that ever happened to you. It may be an apparently small experience that carries a surprisingly strong emotional charge.

We aren't trying to dig around in your past for the sake of it. We're trying to understand the connections between **past experiences, present difficulties and the way you imagine the future**.

## Then comes the processing

Once we've identified a target memory and you're sufficiently prepared, we can begin the processing part of EMDR.

You'll bring the memory to mind along with the thoughts, feelings and physical sensations connected to it. I'll then use bilateral stimulation – which might involve guided eye movements, but can also involve other forms of stimulation – while you notice what happens.

The important thing is that you aren't being asked to force yourself to relive the experience.

You don't have to describe every detail.

Instead, we pay attention to what comes up and allow your brain to make new connections. Sometimes memories change in how they feel. Sometimes new information or perspectives emerge. Sometimes something that has felt very present and threatening starts to feel more like something that happened **then**, rather than something that is happening **now**.

And we keep checking in with how you're doing.

## One foot in the past, one foot in the present

One of the things I think is particularly important about EMDR is remembering that you're sitting in a therapy room **now**, even while we're working with something that happened **then**.

You have one foot in the past and one foot in the present.

You're remembering something difficult, but you're also aware that you're here, now, with someone supporting you.

That sense of dual awareness is part of what makes the work manageable. We don't simply throw you back into an experience and hope for the best.

And if something feels too much, we can slow down, pause or step back.

## EMDR isn't a race

Everyone's journey through EMDR is different.

Some people come with a single event and find that the work progresses relatively quickly. Others have more complicated histories, multiple experiences to work through or current circumstances that need attention first.

The number of sessions isn't something I can predict before getting to know you.

What I can say is that EMDR is structured. There is a plan, but there is also flexibility within that plan.

The aim isn't to rush you through a series of techniques. It's to understand what's troubling you now, identify the experiences that may be contributing to it, make sure you have the resources to deal with the work, and then process those experiences at a pace that feels safe and manageable.

So if you're considering EMDR, you don't need to know exactly where to start.

That's part of my job.

We start with **you, where you are now**, and work from there.
