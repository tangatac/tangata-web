In a great deal of my work as a counsellor, one word comes up again and again: **balance**.

When I first wrote this post in 2019, I used the image of someone walking a tightrope. I still use versions of that idea because it gives us a simple way of thinking about why life can begin to feel unexpectedly difficult.

## The tightrope

Imagine walking a tightrope carrying one of those long balancing poles. Hanging from one end is a bucket containing things that replenish you. Hanging from the other are the demands that draw on you.

The contents will be different for everybody.

Replenishing things might include rest, time outside, movement, creativity, connection, solitude, sleep or doing something simply because you enjoy it.

The other bucket might contain work pressure, caring responsibilities, conflict, uncertainty, illness, difficult memories, financial worries or the thousand small tasks that have to be done.

Even activities we value can belong in either bucket. Helping somebody else can feel deeply nourishing one day and exhausting on another.

The point is not to achieve a perfect fifty-fifty balance. It is to notice when one side has quietly become very heavy.

## Why life can suddenly feel harder

When most of our energy is going out and very little is coming back in, things that we would normally manage can start to feel harder.

That does not necessarily mean anything is 'wrong' with us. It may simply mean that the balance has changed.

Sometimes noticing this is useful in itself. Instead of asking, "Why can't I cope with this?", another question becomes possible:

**"What has been using up my resources, and what has been missing?"**

The NHS's [five steps to mental wellbeing](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/self-help/guides-tools-and-activities/five-steps-to-mental-wellbeing/) includes connection, physical activity, learning, giving and paying attention to the present. I like that these are broad ideas rather than instructions — each person still has to discover what genuinely replenishes them.

## Recovering some room to move

For me, physical activity and being outdoors are often restorative. You may have completely different things.

You can read more about my thoughts on [exercise and mental health](/blog/exercise-and-mental-health/) and [sleep](/blog/benefits-of-sleep-and-learning-to-sleep-well/).

In counselling, this idea can become a starting point rather than a prescription. We can look together at what is drawing heavily on you, what keeps getting pushed aside, and whether there are patterns behind that imbalance.

Sometimes the issue is not that we need to become better at self-care. Sometimes there are relationships, expectations, experiences or beliefs that make it very difficult to give ourselves permission to have needs at all.

That is usually where the conversation becomes more interesting.
