Social anxiety can make everyday situations feel much more threatening than they appear from the outside. Starting conversations, meeting new people, speaking on the phone, eating with others or feeling watched can all become loaded with worry about being judged, embarrassed or getting something wrong.

The NHS describes social anxiety as more than shyness: it can affect confidence, relationships, work, education and ordinary day-to-day activities. [You can read more about social anxiety on the NHS website](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/social-anxiety/).

When I first wrote about this in 2018, the part I kept coming back to was **awareness**. I still think that matters.

## From automatic reactions to noticing

Anxiety can become so familiar that the sequence happens almost automatically: something feels threatening, the body reacts, thoughts race ahead, and avoidance or self-protection follows.

Beginning to notice those patterns does not mean you can simply switch them off. But awareness can create a little more room to understand what is happening and, over time, make choices that were harder to see before.

That work needs to happen at a pace that feels manageable.

## Trust comes before pushing for change

In therapy, I am much less interested in pushing someone to be braver than in understanding what makes a situation feel unsafe in the first place.

For some people, social anxiety is connected with experiences of criticism, rejection, humiliation or bullying. For others, the story is less obvious. Either way, feeling sufficiently safe with a therapist to explore what is happening can be important.

That means there is no requirement to arrive ready to tackle the most frightening situation. We can start with understanding, curiosity and the relationship between us.

Sometimes integrative counselling is the most useful way to work. Where earlier experiences seem to be driving present-day anxiety, [EMDR may also be worth considering](/emdr/anxiety/). We can think about that together rather than deciding in advance that one approach must fit.

## Change does not have to mean becoming someone else

The aim is not to turn a quiet person into an extrovert or to remove every trace of nervousness.

A more useful question might be whether anxiety is stopping you doing things that matter to you, expressing yourself, building relationships or making choices you would otherwise want to make.

If it is, therapy can offer a place to understand those patterns without judgement and work out what greater freedom might look like for you.
