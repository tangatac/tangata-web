Loneliness is not simply the same thing as being alone.

You can feel lonely when you live by yourself, after a bereavement or move, when illness makes it harder to get out, or in the middle of a relationship, friendship group or busy workplace.

The NHS notes that loneliness can affect people of any age and has many different causes. [Its current guidance on loneliness](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/feeling-lonely/) includes talking to someone, joining activities, spending time around other people, volunteering and asking a GP about social prescribing where that might help.

When I first wrote about this in 2020, I was interested in something smaller: the ordinary moments of human contact we can easily overlook.

## Small interactions still count

A conversation with a neighbour. Choosing the staffed checkout and exchanging a few words. Saying hello to somebody you regularly pass. Sending a message because someone came to mind.

None of those things 'fixes loneliness', and we cannot know what somebody else needs simply by looking at them.

But small gestures can still matter. They are tiny reminders that we exist in relation to other people.

For someone who has begun to feel invisible or disconnected, being acknowledged can sometimes carry more weight than the person offering the gesture realises.

## We do not always look lonely

Loneliness can be difficult to spot because people often continue working, socialising and appearing perfectly capable.

It is also possible to have people around you and still feel that nobody really knows you.

That is one reason I am wary of treating loneliness as a problem solved simply by increasing the number of social contacts. What often matters is the **quality** of connection — feeling seen, understood and able to be yourself.

Social anxiety can make reaching for that connection particularly difficult. I have written separately about [social anxiety, safety and choice](/blog/counselling-social-anxiety/).

## When reaching out feels difficult

Advice to "just talk to someone" can sound simple from the outside.

In reality, low self-esteem, shame, previous rejection, difficult relationships or the belief that we would be a burden can make reaching out feel surprisingly risky.

If that is part of what is happening, the first useful step may be much smaller: noticing the loneliness, acknowledging that connection matters to you, or finding one place where you do not have to pretend everything is fine.

Therapy can be one of those places. It does not replace friendship or community, but it can offer a relationship in which there is room to understand what gets in the way of connection and what you need from other people.
