You may have heard of EMDR. Perhaps somebody has mentioned it, your GP or another therapist has suggested looking into it, or you have simply come across the initials and wondered what they actually mean.

The name itself does not make things particularly easy. EMDR stands for **Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing**, which is quite a mouthful.

Fortunately, understanding the basic idea behind EMDR does not require an understanding of all the terminology.

At its simplest, EMDR is a type of psychotherapy that can help people process difficult experiences that continue to affect them in the present.

## When the past is still having an effect

Most of us have difficult experiences during our lives. Usually, given enough time and support, we are able to make sense of what happened and move forward.

Sometimes, however, an experience can continue to have an impact long after the event itself has passed.

You might know, logically, that you are safe but still find yourself feeling anxious in particular situations. You may react strongly to something without quite understanding why. Perhaps you avoid certain places or situations, struggle with a particular fear, or find yourself thinking, *“I know I shouldn't feel like this, but I do.”*

The event may be over. That does not necessarily mean its influence is.

EMDR is a structured therapy that can help people work with experiences that still seem to have a hold over them.

## Is EMDR just for major trauma?

This is a common question.

When people hear the word *trauma*, they may think about a serious car accident, an assault, a natural disaster or military combat. EMDR can certainly be used to help people who have experienced a single traumatic event.

However, trauma is not always one big thing.

Sometimes the difficulties we experience are connected to things that happened repeatedly over time. This might include bullying, growing up in an unpredictable environment, feeling unsafe in relationships, repeated criticism or emotional neglect.

People can sometimes dismiss these experiences because they do not feel dramatic enough to be called trauma.

They might say things like, *“Nothing particularly bad happened to me,”* or *“Other people had it much worse.”*

That may well be true, but comparing experiences does not necessarily tell us much about their impact.

Two people can go through similar experiences and be affected in very different ways. What matters is not simply what happened, but how those experiences affected the person and how they may still be influencing life now.

For example, someone who was repeatedly criticised as a child may grow up knowing, on one level, that they are perfectly capable. Yet they may still find themselves expecting criticism, worrying excessively about getting things wrong or believing that they are not good enough.

EMDR can sometimes help people understand and process experiences that sit behind these patterns.

## How does EMDR work?

EMDR follows a structured approach made up of **eight phases**. These phases guide the therapy from the initial conversations through preparation and processing, and on to reviewing the progress that has been made.

Before beginning the main processing work, time is spent getting to know you and understanding what has brought you to therapy.

This might include looking at what is happening in your life now, relevant experiences from the past and what you would like to be different in the future.

Preparation is also an important part of the process. EMDR therapy is not about sitting down in the first session and immediately being asked to revisit the most difficult thing that has ever happened to you.

The pace of the therapy will depend on the individual. Before moving into more difficult material, it is important that there is a shared understanding of the work and that you have ways of managing strong emotions should they arise.

When it is appropriate to begin processing particular experiences, EMDR uses **bilateral stimulation**. This may involve following a therapist's hand movements with your eyes, tapping or listening to sounds that alternate from one side to the other.

This is often what people have heard about when they hear the term EMDR. But it takes place within a wider therapeutic process that includes understanding your history, preparation, identifying what needs to be worked on, processing experiences and reviewing what has changed.

During EMDR, we may work with memories, thoughts, emotions and physical sensations that are connected to a particular experience.

Everyone's experience of therapy will be different, and the work is adapted to the individual.

## Do I have to talk about everything that happened?

Another understandable concern is whether starting EMDR means having to describe every detail of a difficult experience.

The answer is not necessarily.

As with any therapy, it is important for the therapist to have enough understanding of your experiences and current difficulties to work with you safely and appropriately. However, EMDR does not necessarily depend on giving a detailed, blow-by-blow account of everything that has happened.

The aim is not simply to make someone repeatedly talk through a difficult experience. The focus is on helping the person process experiences that continue to affect them.

For some people, this can make EMDR feel more manageable than they expected.

## What can EMDR help with?

EMDR is perhaps best known for its use with post-traumatic stress, but it can also be used to help with a range of other difficulties.

Depending on the individual and the experiences involved, this may include:

- the effects of a single traumatic event
- anxiety
- phobias
- difficult or distressing memories
- low self-esteem
- the effects of repeated difficult experiences
- relationship difficulties connected to past experiences
- certain unwanted urges or compulsive behaviours

Of course, this does not mean that every difficulty is the result of trauma, or that EMDR is automatically the right approach for everyone.

Therapy is not really a case of finding a problem on a list and matching it to a particular treatment.

Two people may both describe themselves as anxious but have very different reasons for feeling that way. One person's anxiety may be linked to a particular frightening experience. Another's may have developed gradually over many years. Someone else may be struggling primarily with something happening in their life right now.

Part of the job of a therapist is to understand the individual rather than simply treating the label.

## Looking at the past, present and future

One of the things I find particularly helpful about EMDR is the way it can look at all three.

The past may help us understand where certain beliefs, reactions or patterns began.

The present helps us identify where those patterns are still having an impact.

And therapy can also look towards the future: how you would like to respond differently and how you might approach situations that previously felt difficult.

This does not mean spending therapy endlessly digging around in childhood. Nor is the past automatically the explanation for everything that happens in the present.

Sometimes, however, understanding where something began can help us understand why it still has such an effect.

EMDR provides a structured way of making those connections and working towards change.

## Is EMDR right for me?

That is difficult to answer without knowing something about you and what you are looking for help with.

EMDR can be a very effective therapy, but it is not a magic solution and it is not necessarily the right approach for every person or every difficulty.

The best place to start is usually with a conversation.

We can talk about what is happening for you, what you would like to change and whether EMDR might be an appropriate approach. Sometimes another form of therapy may be more suitable. In some cases, different approaches may be used together.

You do not need to arrive knowing exactly what is wrong, where it came from or which therapy you need.

That is something we can begin to work out together.
