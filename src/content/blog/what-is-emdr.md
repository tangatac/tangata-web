If you've been offered EMDR, or read the word somewhere and felt none the wiser, you're in good company. It has an unwieldy name and a slightly unusual method — so let me explain it plainly, the way I would if you were sitting across from me.

EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing. It was developed by the psychologist Francine Shapiro in the late 1980s, and it's now one of the therapies recommended by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the World Health Organization for post-traumatic stress. In other words, it isn't a fringe idea — it's a well-researched approach with a lot of evidence behind it.

## Why some memories get "stuck"

Most of the time, your brain processes experiences and files them away sensibly. You can recall them, but they don't hijack the present. When something overwhelming happens, though, that filing can go wrong. The memory gets stored with all its original intensity — the images, the sensations, the beliefs about yourself that came with it — as if frozen at the moment it happened.

That's why a sound, a smell or an offhand comment can suddenly drop you back into a feeling you thought was long behind you. The memory hasn't been processed; it's still live.

> EMDR doesn't erase what happened. It helps your brain finish the job of filing it away, so it loses its grip.

## What the eye movements are for

Here's the part that sounds strange until you understand it. During EMDR, I'll ask you to bring a difficult memory briefly to mind while you follow a gentle side-to-side movement with your eyes — sometimes a light, sometimes my hand. If eye movements don't suit you, we can use soft taps or tones instead. It's called bilateral stimulation, because it alternates left and right.

This left-right rhythm is thought to work in a similar way to what your brain does naturally during dreaming sleep, when it processes the events of the day. Holding the memory in mind while the attention gently shifts side to side seems to help the brain do what it couldn't at the time: reprocess the experience so it settles into the past, where it belongs.

## What actually happens in a session

EMDR follows a clear, structured process, and we never rush into the difficult part. Early sessions are about getting to know each other, understanding your history, and making sure you have ways to feel grounded and safe. Only when you're ready do we begin reprocessing — and even then, you stay in control the whole way through.

One thing that surprises people: you don't have to describe the memory in detail, or relive it out loud. EMDR works with what your mind notices as we go, so you can keep as much private as you wish. We check in constantly, and we always close a session in a settled, steady place — never in the middle of something raw.

### What it can help with

The evidence is strongest for trauma and post-traumatic stress, but in practice EMDR can help with a wide range of things that have their roots in difficult experiences:

- Trauma and PTSD, including single incidents and longer-standing experiences
- Anxiety and panic that keep returning
- Specific phobias
- Grief and bereavement that feel stuck
- Low self-esteem tied to earlier experiences

<div class="disclaimer">Everyone's experience is different, and EMDR isn't the right fit for every person or every situation. The number of sessions varies, and we'd always decide together whether it's a good path for you. Nothing here is a substitute for personalised advice.</div>

## Is it right for you?

If any of this resonates — if there's something you can't quite move past, however big or small it seems — the best next step is simply to talk. There's no pressure and no commitment: just a chance to ask questions and see how it feels.
