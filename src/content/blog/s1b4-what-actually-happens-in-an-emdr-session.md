If you’ve seen EMDR online, you’ve probably seen the bit everyone recognises: someone sitting opposite a therapist, following their fingers from side to side.

But what actually happens?

There’s quite a bit more to an EMDR session than moving your eyes. By the time we get to the eye movements, we’ve already spent time getting to know you, understanding what is troubling you now, identifying where those difficulties may have come from and making sure you have the resources to do the work safely.

But once we have identified a particular memory to work with, what does the actual processing look like?

## First, we connect with the memory

We don't simply launch into a difficult memory and hope for the best.

Once we have identified a specific memory that seems important in relation to the difficulties you're experiencing now, I'll guide you through identifying some key parts of it.

We might look at **what happened**, the particular image or moment that represents the worst part of the memory, and what you believe about yourself when you think about it.

Often this is a negative belief such as:

*“I'm not good enough.”*

*“It's my fault.”*

*“I'm powerless.”*

*“I'm not safe.”*

We also pay attention to the emotions and physical sensations connected to the memory. Perhaps you notice fear, shame, anger or sadness. You might feel it as a tight chest, a knot in your stomach, tension in your shoulders or a heavy feeling somewhere in your body.

So, before we start, we have a fairly clear picture of what this memory means to you **now**.

Then we begin the processing.

## And this is where the eye movements come in

You bring the memory to mind and then follow my fingers with your eyes as they move from side to side.

I'll usually ask you to notice the memory, the feelings and sensations that are there, and then allow your attention to move wherever it naturally goes.

We typically do a set of around **20–30 eye movements**, although the exact number isn't rigid.

Then we stop.

I'll ask you something like:

**“What do you notice now?”**

You might say that you have another image, a different thought, a feeling, a physical sensation or perhaps something you weren't expecting at all.

We then do another set of movements.

And another.

With pauses in between, I check what is happening and allow you to notice whatever comes up.

This is important because EMDR isn't about me telling you what you should be thinking or feeling. I'm not interpreting every thought or trying to steer you towards a particular conclusion.

I'm interested in what your brain is doing with the memory.

Sometimes something changes quite quickly. Sometimes the memory seems to move around, become less vivid or bring up another aspect of the experience. Sometimes you notice something in your body that you hadn't noticed before.

We keep working with it until the memory no longer has the same level of disturbance.

## What does “processed” actually mean?

It doesn't mean you've forgotten what happened.

That's a really important distinction.

You can still remember the event. You know that it happened. But the memory doesn't necessarily feel as though it is happening **right now**.

The emotional charge can reduce.

The physical reaction can settle.

And the meaning you have attached to what happened can change.

This is one of the reasons EMDR can feel quite different from simply talking about a difficult experience. We aren't just discussing what happened; we're working with the memory and the way it is currently stored and experienced.

## Then we look at what you'd rather believe

Once the disturbance connected to the memory has reduced, we turn our attention to a more adaptive or helpful belief.

This is sometimes called the **positive cognition**.

For example, perhaps the original belief was:

**“I'm powerless.”**

The more adaptive belief might be:

**“I have choices now.”**

Or perhaps:

**“I'm not good enough”** becomes **“I am good enough.”**

Or:

**“I'm not safe”** becomes **“I am safe now.”**

The important thing is that the positive belief isn't something I simply choose for you. It needs to feel relevant and meaningful to you.

We then work with that belief too, helping it feel more true and more connected to the memory.

## And then we check the body

Finally, we do a quick check of what is happening physically.

If you think about the memory now, does anything still feel uncomfortable or unsettled in your body?

This matters because sometimes your thoughts can have moved on while your body is still saying, *“Hang on, I'm not convinced.”*

Ideally, the emotional and physical response becomes more settled and the positive belief feels more natural.

Again, we're not trying to pretend that what happened was okay.

We're helping your present-day self respond to something that happened in the past.

## What if something else comes up?

This is actually quite common.

During processing, your mind might bring up another memory, another person, a completely different image or a physical sensation.

That's okay.

We don't necessarily need to go back to the original memory and force it to stay there. We work with what emerges and see where the processing takes us.

And sometimes the memory changes in ways that are difficult to predict.

You might suddenly remember something you hadn't thought about for years. You might realise that a belief you've carried about yourself doesn't fit anymore. Or you might notice that something which has triggered a strong reaction for years simply doesn't seem to have the same impact.

## It isn't a race

One thing I always want people to understand is that EMDR isn't about getting through as many memories as possible.

Some memories process relatively quickly. Others take more time.

And sometimes we need to stop, go back to preparation or make sure you're sufficiently grounded before continuing.

That's why the earlier work around safety and stabilisation matters so much.

When we're processing a difficult experience, you need to be able to have **one foot in the past and one foot firmly in the present**.

You're remembering something that happened then, while also knowing that you're here, now, sitting in the therapy room.

That distinction is fundamental.

## So what does an EMDR session actually feel like?

It can be difficult to describe because everyone's experience is different.

Some people notice images moving through their mind. Some notice thoughts changing. Some notice emotions or physical sensations. Some experience very little consciously and simply notice afterwards that something feels different.

And sometimes it's surprisingly ordinary.

A memory that used to make your stomach drop doesn't.

A situation that you always avoided feels manageable.

A thought about yourself that you've carried for years suddenly doesn't seem quite so convincing.

The eye movements are certainly an important part of EMDR. But they are not the whole therapy.

They are part of a structured process that starts with understanding what is happening for you, identifying what needs attention, making sure you are ready to process it, and then carefully working with the memories, beliefs, emotions and physical responses that are keeping you stuck.

The aim isn't to change your past.

**It's to change the way your past is affecting your present.**
