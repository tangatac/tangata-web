Most of us know that sleep matters. Knowing that and actually going to bed when another task, programme or scroll through a phone is available are two different things.

When I first wrote this post in 2019, I was thinking about the way poor sleep can affect how manageable everything else feels. That still seems important, although I would now be more cautious about turning one person's experience into a formula for everybody.

The NHS says that adults need different amounts of sleep, with an average of around seven to nine hours. Regular sleep problems can affect mood, concentration and everyday functioning. [Its current insomnia guidance is a useful starting point](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insomnia/).

## There is no single recipe

Sleep is influenced by many things: stress, anxiety, mood, pain, health conditions, medication, caffeine, alcohol, shift work, the sleep environment and simply what is happening in life.

That is one reason generic advice can sometimes feel frustrating. A perfect evening routine is not much help if your mind is racing because something important feels unresolved.

Still, small habits can be useful. The NHS suggests keeping reasonably regular sleep and wake times, giving yourself time to wind down, making the bedroom comfortable, being physically active during the day and being mindful of caffeine and screen use close to bedtime.

## A personal observation about mental and physical tiredness

I have long had a personal sense that I sleep better when both my mind and my body have had something useful to do during the day.

I do not think that is a rule, and it certainly is not a treatment for insomnia. It is simply one way I notice balance in my own life. Movement can help me settle, while meaningful mental activity gives my attention somewhere constructive to go.

That links with my older reflection on [exercise and mental health](/blog/exercise-and-mental-health/) and with the broader idea of [balance in mental health](/blog/balance-in-mental-health-the-elixir-of-life/).

## When sleep problems are telling us something

Sometimes sleep improves with fairly ordinary adjustments. Sometimes it does not.

If sleep problems have been going on for months, are making everyday life hard to manage, or you think there may be a medical or sleep disorder behind them, NHS guidance recommends speaking to a GP. Treatment can include looking at the underlying cause and, for some people, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia.

And sometimes difficulty sleeping is one sign that life simply feels too full, anxious or unsettled. Therapy will not make every sleep problem disappear, but it can provide space to understand what is keeping your system busy and what else may need attention.
