When people hear that EMDR is used to process difficult or traumatic experiences, it’s easy to imagine that the therapy is simply about going back into the past and dealing with what happened.

And yes, the past is an important part of EMDR. But it isn’t the whole story.

One of the things I like about the EMDR approach is that it looks at the **past, the present and the future**. The aim isn't just to help someone feel differently about something that happened years ago. It’s also about helping them respond differently to what happens now – and feel more prepared for what might happen in the future.

## Starting with the past

By the time we get to processing memories, we've usually spent some time understanding what is happening for the person in their life now.

Perhaps they are experiencing anxiety, avoiding certain situations, struggling with relationships, feeling overwhelmed, or reacting particularly strongly to something that other people seem to cope with more easily.

We then work backwards to identify the experiences that may be contributing to these difficulties.

Sometimes there is an obvious event: a car accident, an assault, a bereavement or another frightening experience. Sometimes it is less straightforward. Difficulties may have developed over many years, perhaps through repeated experiences of criticism, rejection, bullying, neglect or feeling unsafe.

We aren't trying to produce a perfect autobiography. Instead, we're looking for the memories that seem to be connected to the difficulties someone is experiencing now.

Once we've identified these, we can work through them using EMDR, helping the brain to process experiences that still feel stuck or unresolved.

The aim isn't to erase the memory. The event still happened and the person can still remember it. But the hope is that it becomes something that happened **then**, rather than something that continues to feel as though it is happening **now**.

## Then we look at the present

Processing the relevant memories from the past can make a significant difference, but sometimes something in the present continues to cause a reaction.

You might have processed the original experience of being bullied, for example, but still find that someone making a critical comment at work produces a surprisingly strong response.

Or perhaps the original accident has been processed, but getting into a car still produces a little jolt of anxiety.

These present-day triggers can also be worked with.

We can identify the situation that is activating, notice what thoughts, feelings or sensations are coming up, and use EMDR to process the remaining disturbance.

It's worth remembering that the goal isn't necessarily to make someone completely indifferent to everything that happens.

Some things **should** matter. A difficult conversation, an argument with someone we care about or a stressful situation at work can quite reasonably make us feel uncomfortable.

The difference is between having an appropriate emotional response to something happening now and finding that your nervous system is reacting as though an old threat is happening all over again.

## What about the future?

There's another part of this that I find particularly useful.

Sometimes people have dealt with the experiences that contributed to their difficulties and the triggers have reduced, but they still don't feel confident about how to handle situations in everyday life.

Perhaps they've spent years avoiding conflict, saying yes when they mean no, putting other people's needs before their own, or becoming overwhelmed when things don't go according to plan.

These aren't necessarily traumatic memories that need processing. They can be more about **skills, confidence and learning a different way of responding**.

This is where EMDR's future template work can be particularly helpful.

We can think about a situation that the person expects might be difficult in the future and imagine how they would like to deal with it.

For example:

- How would I like to respond if someone criticises me?

- What would I like to do if I start feeling overwhelmed?

- How would I like to handle a difficult conversation?

- What would it be like to set a boundary?

- How would I like to respond if I make a mistake?

We can then mentally rehearse that new response using EMDR.

It isn't about pretending that everything will go perfectly. Life doesn't work like that. It's about giving the brain an opportunity to practise a different response before the situation actually occurs.

## From surviving to responding

This past–present–future approach is one reason I think it's helpful to see EMDR as more than simply a treatment for traumatic memories.

We can look at **what happened**, how it is still affecting you **now**, and how you would like to respond **in the future**.

Perhaps a person has spent a long time feeling that their reactions are out of their control. They might understand intellectually that something happened years ago, but their body still reacts as though the danger is present.

Processing the past can help reduce that old alarm. Working with present-day triggers can help settle the things that are still activating them. And future template work can help build confidence in dealing with situations differently.

The goal isn't to turn someone into a completely different person. It's to help them have more choice.

Instead of automatically avoiding, shutting down, becoming overwhelmed or going into fight-or-flight, they may be able to pause and think: **What do I actually want to do here?**

That's ultimately what we're working towards.

Not forgetting the past.

Not pretending difficult things won't happen again.

But being able to recognise that something happened, understand how it has affected you, and have more freedom in deciding how you respond to life now.

And perhaps that's one of the most useful ways of thinking about EMDR: **processing what was, responding to what is, and preparing for what comes next.**
